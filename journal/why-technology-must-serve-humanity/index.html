Why Technology Must Serve Humanity

Technology is one of the most powerful tools we have ever made. The question is not whether it is powerful, but what it is for.

By Kamal Dharma

Technology is one of the most powerful tools humanity has ever made. In a single lifetime we have gone from waiting weeks for a letter to speaking with anyone on the planet in a second. We can map a genome, steer a satellite and teach a machine to read. None of that is in doubt. What is in doubt is what all that power is for.

For much of the last two decades, the honest answer has often been convenience, profit or automation. Those are not bad things. Convenience gives people time back. Profit keeps companies alive and people employed. Automation removes work nobody wants to do. But when they become the whole purpose, technology starts to optimise for the wrong things. We get products designed to capture attention rather than give it back, systems that replace people rather than strengthen them, and tools that are brilliant at the easy problems while the hard ones wait.

I believe technology should be measured by a simpler question: does it help people thrive? Does it help someone learn faster? Create more? Connect more deeply with the people and the world around them? Solve a problem that was too hard before? If the answer is yes, it is worth building. If the answer is no, cleverness alone does not justify it.

This is not an abstract position for me. When I build software for real-estate teams, construction sites or schools, I see the difference between a system that serves people and one that merely processes them. A good CRM does not just record a sale; it helps a salesperson understand a family looking for their first home. A good construction platform does not just track delays; it helps an engineer spot a problem before it becomes a danger. A good education system does not just store marks; it helps a teacher notice the child who is quietly falling behind.

Serving humanity also means remembering who is left out. Much of the world still meets technology as something built elsewhere, for someone else, in a language and price that do not fit. Building for humanity means building for those people too: for slower networks, for first-time users, for places where a mistake has real consequences. Accessibility is not a feature to add later. It is part of the purpose.

And it means remembering that humanity is not separate from the rest of life. We depend on forests, rivers, soil and the animals that share them. Technology that helps us extract faster but leaves the planet poorer is not progress; it is borrowing against our children. The same tools that build smarter cities can monitor ecosystems, protect endangered species and help us restore what we have damaged.

None of this happens by accident. Technology reflects the intentions of the people who build it. If builders choose to measure success by human outcomes, by lives improved, problems solved and time well spent, then technology will slowly bend in that direction. If we do not choose, the defaults will choose for us.

So this is the choice I try to make each day, in every product, research project and plan for the future: to build systems and ideas that move us towards a world where technology elevates people rather than distracts them, and protects the planet rather than consumes it.

Technology must serve humanity. Not because it is fashionable to say, but because nothing else is worth the power we now hold.
